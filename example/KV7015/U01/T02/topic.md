# What makes a question answerable

- Clear terms
- Available evidence
- A realistic scope

> **Say:** In the previous topic we narrowed a broad area down to a question. Now we ask a harder thing. Can the question actually be answered? An answerable question has clear terms, relies on evidence that exists or can be collected, and has a scope that fits the time and resources you really have.

---

## Defining your terms

- Every key word needs a definition
- Operational definitions

> **Say:** Every important word in your question needs a definition that someone else could apply. If your question mentions wellbeing, what exactly do you mean by it, and how would two different researchers recognise it? An operational definition turns an abstract idea into something observable. Without one, two studies of the same question can reach opposite answers.

---

## Is the evidence available?

- Can you collect it?
- Can you get permission?
- Does it already exist?

> **Say:** Next, think about evidence. Some questions are perfectly clear but impossible to answer because the data cannot be collected, or cannot be collected ethically. Others can be answered from data that already exists, which saves a great deal of time. Ask where the evidence would come from before you fall in love with the question.

---

## Scope and resources

- Time
- Access
- Skills

> **Say:** Scope is where many good questions fail. A question about every school in the country may be answerable in principle, but not by one researcher in a few months. Be honest about your time, your access to participants, and the skills you already have or can learn. Trim the question until it fits.

---

## Descriptive or causal?

- What is happening?
- Why is it happening?

> **Say:** It also helps to know what kind of question you are asking. Descriptive questions ask what is happening. Causal questions ask why. Causal questions are usually much harder to answer well, because you have to rule out other explanations. Neither kind is better, but they call for very different research designs.

---

## A quick checklist

- Terms defined
- Evidence located
- Scope trimmed
- Type identified

> **Say:** To finish, run your question through a quick checklist. Are the terms defined? Do you know where the evidence will come from? Does the scope fit your resources? Do you know whether it is descriptive or causal? If you can answer yes to all four, your question is ready for the next stage.
